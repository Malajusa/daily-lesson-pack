# Universal Mathematics Instruction Canon

## Status and authority

This is the mandatory instructional standard for all student-facing Mathematics content generated by the Daily Lesson Pack.

Apply the complete canon to the main Mathematics lesson. Apply its mathematical-accuracy, terminology, representation, task-integrity and answer-integrity rules to Mathematics warm-ups and Morning Work, while preserving those components as retrieval rather than new teaching.

An explicit current user instruction may change the lesson mode, number of slides, representation or task structure. It does not override mathematical accuracy, truthful representation, readable notation or the requirement that an answer address the task actually asked.

Use these requirement words consistently:

- **MUST** and **MUST NOT** are release conditions.
- **SHOULD** is the default unless the lesson purpose provides a documented reason to vary it.
- **MAY** identifies an available option rather than a requirement.

## 1. Internal Mathematics planning contract

Before generating the main Mathematics lesson, record an internal planning contract containing:

1. the curriculum concept and its boundary;
2. the lesson mode;
3. the one central new mathematical idea, or `none` for review/assessment;
4. the prerequisite knowledge to retrieve;
5. the quantities, units, properties, structures or relationships students must notice;
6. what changes and what remains invariant;
7. the technical vocabulary and notation students will use;
8. the selected representation or representations and why each reveals the intended structure;
9. the principal anticipated misconception;
10. the highest independent demand students will face;
11. the evidence the exit check will collect.

The planning contract is teacher-facing or internal. Do not place it on student slides.

The main Mathematics component acceptance record MUST state that each item has been checked. A missing planning contract or unsubstantiated acceptance result is a component failure.

## 2. Classify the lesson before designing it

Use one primary lesson mode:

- **Concept introduction:** establish meaning, properties, representations and initial connections.
- **Procedure development:** explain why a method works, model it and develop efficient use.
- **Fluency development:** retrieve and practise already taught knowledge for accuracy and efficiency.
- **Reasoning:** compare, justify, classify, generalise, prove or evaluate.
- **Problem solving:** interpret a situation, select useful mathematics, construct a solution path and evaluate the result.
- **Review/assessment:** determine what students can do independently without teaching prompts that reveal the method.

The mode determines the balance of explanation, modelling, guidance and independent work. Do not force every lesson into identical proportions.

A lesson MUST NOT combine several substantial new concepts merely because they share a broad topic label. Unless the lesson is explicitly review, consolidation or extension, it SHOULD introduce one central new mathematical idea.

## 3. Meaning before procedure

A newly introduced procedure MUST be grounded in mathematical meaning before it is presented as a sequence of steps.

The explanation MUST identify, as relevant:

- what quantities or objects are represented;
- the unit being counted or measured;
- the relationship between the quantities;
- what changes during the operation;
- what remains invariant;
- why the procedure preserves the intended mathematical relationship.

A mnemonic or shorthand MAY support recall after meaning has been established. It MUST NOT replace the mathematical explanation.

Do not use the following as primary explanations:

- `borrow`, `steal` or `take one from next door` without place-value regrouping;
- `move the decimal point` without explaining powers of ten and place value;
- `add the top numbers and keep the bottom number` without explaining common fractional units;
- `flip and multiply` without explaining the reciprocal relationship;
- `length times width` without connecting the formula to rows and columns of square units;
- `bring it across and change the sign` without maintaining equivalence;
- any rule that students could apply while misunderstanding the quantities involved.

Use accurate mathematical vocabulary while making its meaning explicit in student-friendly language.

## 4. Select representations for mathematical purpose

Every representation MUST be selected because it reveals a relevant structure, relationship, quantity or property. Decorative mathematical imagery MUST NOT substitute for a representation.

Use the minimum set of representations needed to establish understanding. Each additional representation MUST add meaning or make a connection; it must not merely repeat the same information decoratively.

Commonly useful representation families include:

| Concept family | Suitable representations |
| --- | --- |
| Counting and place value | collections, ten-frames, bundling models, base-ten materials, place-value charts, number lines |
| Addition and subtraction | part-part-whole models, comparison bars, number lines, place-value models, equations |
| Multiplication | equal groups, arrays, area models, scaling diagrams, number lines |
| Division | sharing models, grouping models, arrays, bar models, inverse equations |
| Fractions | fraction bars, area models, set models, number lines, ratio tables |
| Decimals and percentages | place-value charts, hundred grids, number lines, ratio tables, double number lines |
| Ratio and proportion | ratio tables, double number lines, bar models, scale diagrams |
| Algebra and patterns | tables, balance models, function machines, diagrams, graphs, symbolic equations |
| Measurement | iterated units, scaled drawings, arrays of units, formula diagrams, conversion tables |
| Geometry | accurate diagrams, grids, constructions, transformations, nets |
| Statistics | tables, dot plots, column graphs, line graphs, distributions |
| Probability | organised lists, tables, tree diagrams, sample-space grids, simulations |

This table guides selection; it does not make every listed representation compulsory.

## 5. Mathematical exactness of visuals

Any visual used as mathematical evidence MUST be exact enough to support the intended conclusion.

Verify, where relevant:

- equal intervals on number lines and axes;
- correct direction and landing points;
- correct quantities and place values in base-ten models;
- correct rows, columns, group sizes and totals in arrays;
- correct dimensions, partitions and square/cubic units in measurement models;
- equal fraction parts, correct denominators and correct shading;
- correct angles, parallelism, perpendicularity, symmetry and congruence;
- correct coordinates, axes, scale and plotted points;
- correct graph values, categories, intervals and labels;
- a complete and correctly weighted sample space;
- correct signs, values and equivalence in algebra tiles or balance models.

A mathematically misleading visual blocks release even when the accompanying equation is correct.

Photographs MAY establish context. They MUST NOT be treated as mathematical models unless their quantity, partition, scale and perspective accurately represent the mathematics. A photograph that appears measurable or partitioned must not contradict the stated values.

Prefer flat, editable, mathematically exact diagrams when perspective, shadows or decorative effects could make equality, scale or correspondence harder to judge.

## 6. Align representation, language and notation

When a visual model, verbal explanation and symbolic statement appear together, they MUST represent the same quantities, units and relationships.

The slide MUST make the important correspondence explicit rather than assuming that students will infer it. Examples include:

- each row in an array represents one equal group;
- each jump on a number line represents a stated unit or change;
- each region of an area model corresponds to a factor or partial product;
- each side of a balance corresponds to one side of an equation;
- each table row corresponds to a point on a graph;
- each sample-space entry represents one possible outcome.

When colour identifies corresponding quantities, use the same colour consistently across the model, language and notation. Colour MUST NOT be the only means of identifying the connection.

Use semantic colour according to `references/semantic-colour-standard.md`. Do not colour objects merely to make the slide look more varied.

## 7. Use precise vocabulary and notation

Student-friendly explanation MUST support technical vocabulary rather than replace it.

Use the correct term, then explain it plainly. Examples include:

- numerator, denominator and fractional unit;
- factor, multiple and product;
- dividend, divisor, quotient and remainder;
- expression, equation and inequality;
- vertex, edge, face, parallel, perpendicular, congruent and symmetrical;
- scale, interval, axis, category and frequency;
- outcome, event, sample space and likelihood.

Do not replace a known mathematical term with a vague label such as `top number`, `bottom number`, `answer number`, `times number` or `number pattern` when a more precise term is required for the concept.

Notation MUST be readable, conventional and internally consistent. Use properly formatted symbols and stacked fractions where practical for projected instruction. Keep question numbering visually separate from mathematical expressions so that labels cannot be misread as decimals, mixed numbers or coefficients.

## 8. Teaching sequence and response frequency

For a new or fragile concept, the default sequence is:

1. retrieve the specific prerequisite knowledge;
2. establish the concept and intended relationship;
3. model one complete example from start to finish;
4. check understanding immediately;
5. complete a genuinely interactive guided example;
6. provide guided practice with fading scaffolds;
7. provide independent application at an already modelled demand;
8. collect reasoning, application or exit evidence aligned to the intended concept.

The general Mathematics warm-up remains separate from prerequisite checking and from the main lesson.

Do not present a long passive run of explanation slides. During concept introduction or procedure development, students SHOULD make an observable response after no more than two consecutive explanation/model slides unless an explicit current instruction requires another structure.

An observable response may be oral, gestural, mini-whiteboard, manipulative-based, selected-response or written. It must require students to process the mathematics rather than merely repeat a displayed answer.

## 9. Genuine gradual release

An `I do` slide models the thinking and process explicitly.

A `We do` slide MUST require student participation. It MUST NOT already display the completed answer, final landing point, completed model or all working before students have an opportunity to respond.

A `You do` task MUST be solvable using concepts, representations and demands that have already been modelled, unless it is clearly identified as an extension.

Scaffolds SHOULD fade deliberately, for example:

1. complete model and explanation;
2. complete model with one missing step;
3. partially completed model;
4. strategy prompt without completed working;
5. independent strategy selection and execution;
6. application in a less familiar context.

Do not move directly from a fully completed example to unsupported work that adds a new operation, representation, number type, number of steps, reading demand or reasoning demand.

## 10. Align modelled and independent demand

Before release, compare the hardest independent task with the teaching sequence.

They MUST align in all relevant dimensions:

- mathematical concept;
- operation or relationship;
- representation;
- number type and magnitude;
- number of steps;
- required strategy selection;
- language demand;
- reasoning demand;
- expected student output.

Independent practice MUST NOT introduce a new conceptual demand that has not been modelled or explicitly marked as extension.

When independent practice includes more than one operation or method at a given complexity, each must first be modelled at that complexity.

## 11. Question and answer integrity

A student question slide MUST contain the task, required information and permitted scaffold, but MUST NOT reveal the answer through completed working, answer-coloured objects, a final landing point, an already completed model or a teaching-only cue.

The corresponding answer slide MUST:

- answer the exact question asked;
- show sufficient working or reasoning for the lesson purpose;
- include any representation required by the prompt;
- fulfil every command such as explain, compare, justify, label, prove, draw or write an equation;
- acknowledge alternative valid methods or answers where relevant;
- make the primary answer visually dominant;
- highlight meaningful mathematical changes rather than adding only a tick.

If a question asks students to explain with a model, a verbal answer without the model fails.

## 12. Purposeful variation and scaffold fading

Practice items SHOULD vary one mathematically important feature at a time so students notice the target structure.

Useful dimensions of variation include:

- orientation or arrangement;
- location of the unknown;
- magnitude or number type;
- presence of zero or one;
- representation;
- context;
- number of steps;
- requirement to select a strategy.

Do not increase difficulty by changing several unrelated features simultaneously unless the task is deliberately testing transfer.

Avoid repetitive sets that differ only in surface numbers when the lesson goal is conceptual discrimination or strategy selection.

## 13. Examples, non-examples and misconceptions

Where a concept is defined by properties or boundaries, include a carefully selected example and non-example when this will clarify the distinction.

A misconception task MUST use plausible student reasoning. It MUST NOT be so obviously impossible that students can reject it without engaging with the target concept.

A misconception answer SHOULD identify:

1. the mistaken assumption or step;
2. the mathematical property, unit or relationship it violates;
3. the correct reasoning;
4. a supporting representation where this improves understanding.

Do not require a misconception task in every lesson. Include one when the misconception is common, consequential and directly related to the current focus.

## 14. Distinguish calculation, reasoning and problem solving

Label and design a task according to its actual mathematical demand.

- **Calculation:** apply a known process to obtain a result.
- **Reasoning:** explain, justify, compare, classify, generalise, prove or evaluate.
- **Problem solving:** determine what mathematics is needed, construct a solution pathway and evaluate the result.

A routine calculation does not become problem solving merely because it is written as a word problem.

A reasoning task must require reasoning in the student output. Do not add `explain why` mechanically to a warm-up or fluency task when the additional language demand does not serve the lesson purpose.

## 15. Context design

Use a context only when it improves meaning, interpretation or application.

A context SHOULD:

- give a genuine reason for the mathematics;
- make the quantity or relationship meaningful;
- use familiar information or provide the necessary background;
- avoid unnecessary reading load;
- preserve mathematical accuracy.

Australian and Western Australian contexts MAY be preferred when they fit naturally. Do not force a local context when it creates an awkward or inaccurate problem.

A context image MUST NOT contradict the quantities in the text or imply measurements, partitions or scales that are not mathematically valid.

## 16. Visual hierarchy and cognitive load

Each slide MUST have one dominant instructional purpose.

The hierarchy SHOULD make this order clear where applicable:

1. lesson phase or task type;
2. main instruction, question or mathematical statement;
3. representation or working;
4. supporting explanation.

The mathematical content must dominate decoration. Do not preserve oversized clouds, speech bubbles, emojis, photographs, borders or cards while shrinking the actual question, representation or reasoning.

Apply:

- `references/slide-deck-quality-standards.md`;
- `references/semantic-colour-standard.md`;
- `references/panel-containment-standard.md`.

Use blank space to improve focus and separation, not to leave important content unnecessarily small.

## 17. Differentiation

Differentiate through mathematical access and demand, not merely through quantity.

Possible adjustments include:

- concrete, pictorial or symbolic representation;
- provided or student-selected strategy;
- familiar or less familiar context;
- one-step or multi-step structure;
- calculation, explanation, comparison or generalisation;
- amount of scaffold;
- level of abstraction.

Do not define higher attainment as completing the same task with more working or checking. Do not make an extension dependent on completing a lower task when the students should be able to enter at an appropriate level.

## 18. Component-specific application

### Main Mathematics lesson

Apply the complete canon, including the planning contract, lesson-mode classification, scope control, meaning-before-procedure requirement, representation choice, gradual release, model-to-practice alignment and exit evidence.

### Mathematics warm-up

Keep the warm-up as retrieval of taught content. Apply mathematical accuracy, exact representations, precise terminology, question/answer integrity, projected readability and plausible distractor rules. Do not use the warm-up to introduce a new procedure or require extended justification unless the user explicitly requests reasoning.

### Morning Work

Keep the task independently executable from the projected slide and completed in books. Apply mathematical accuracy, precise vocabulary and unambiguous output requirements. Do not add teacher-dependent modelling or new conceptual teaching.

### Review and assessment

Do not reveal the strategy through prompts, colour coding, completed models or step labels when independent selection is part of the intended evidence. Keep all required information available and remove irrelevant scaffolds.

## 19. Release-blocking Mathematics QA gate

The Mathematics component or complete pack MUST fail if any of the following is true:

- the curriculum concept or lesson boundary is unclear;
- several substantial new concepts are combined without an explicit review/consolidation purpose;
- a new procedure is presented without establishing mathematical meaning;
- technical vocabulary is replaced by vague or inaccurate terminology;
- a mathematical visual contains incorrect quantity, scale, interval, partition, coordinate, property, label or correspondence;
- a decorative image is used as if it were mathematical evidence;
- the visual, language and notation represent different quantities or relationships;
- a `We do` task already displays the completed solution;
- independent work introduces an unmodelled conceptual demand;
- a question slide reveals the answer;
- an answer omits a required explanation, representation, comparison, proof, label or equation;
- a misconception is implausible or diagnostically useless;
- a task labelled reasoning or problem solving is only routine calculation;
- a context or photograph contradicts the mathematics;
- the exit check measures only use of a representation rather than the intended concept;
- valid alternative answers are marked wrong without a stated restriction;
- important notation, diagrams or reasoning are too small for whole-class projection;
- the component acceptance record does not substantiate the planning-contract and canon checks.

A Mathematics component passes only when its content, representations, sequence, student actions, answers and rendered slides all satisfy the applicable parts of this canon.
